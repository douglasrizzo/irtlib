Introduction#

irtlib is a Python package for item response theory (IRT), the models that relate an examinee’s responses to the items of a test to the examinee’s ability and to the items’ parameters. It calibrates items, estimating their parameters from the responses of a sample of examinees; it estimates the abilities of examinees from their responses to calibrated items; and it checks how well a calibration fits the responses. This page introduces the models and the methods, and ends with a map of irtlib’s parts. Terms and Notation lists the terms and symbols these pages use.

What a Test Measures#

A test measures an examinee’s ability, written \(\theta\): a characteristic that cannot be observed directly, such as knowledge of a subject. What can be observed are the examinee’s responses to the test’s items, each scored correct or incorrect, and item response theory infers the ability from them as a number on a continuous scale.

The responses of a sample of examinees form a response matrix, with one row per examinee and one column per item. It holds 1 for a correct response, 0 for an incorrect one, and NaN where the examinee did not answer the item, as when examinees take forms of a test that share only some of their items. With \(N\) examinees and \(M\) items, \(u_{ij}\) is examinee \(j\)’s response to item \(i\).

The Logistic Models#

Under the four-parameter logistic model (4PL), an examinee of ability \(\theta\) answers item \(i\) correctly with probability

\[P_i(\theta) = c_i + \frac{d_i - c_i}{1 + e^{-a_i(\theta - b_i)}}\]

[Magis, 2013, p. 306, Equation 1], the item’s characteristic curve. Its four parameters shape the curve:

  • the discrimination \(a_i\), its slope: how sharply the item separates examinees of lower and higher ability;

  • the difficulty \(b_i\), the ability at which the curve is steepest, on the same scale as \(\theta\);

  • the guessing parameter \(c_i\), the curve’s lower asymptote: the probability that an examinee of very low ability answers the item correctly;

  • the upper asymptote \(d_i\): the probability that an examinee of very high ability answers the item correctly, below 1 for an item that such examinees still miss [Barton and Lord, 1981, p. 2].

The three-parameter model (3PL) fixes every \(d_i = 1\), the two-parameter model (2PL) also every \(c_i = 0\), and the one-parameter model (1PL) also gives every item the same discrimination; the Rasch model is the 1PL with every discrimination equal to 1 [Magis and Raîche, 2012, p. 3]. irtlib writes the models without the scaling constant that some programs multiply each \(a_i\) by (ADR-0001: irtlib Implements Item Response Theory for Unidimensional Dichotomous Items).

Each response also carries information about the ability: the more information a test’s items give at an examinee’s ability, the smaller the standard error of the estimate made from their responses [Magis and Raîche, 2012, p. 4, Equation 3]. The formulas of these quantities are on the Item Response Functions page.

The ability scale has no natural origin or unit. Moving every ability and difficulty by one amount leaves every probability unchanged, and so does stretching the abilities and difficulties by one factor while the discriminations shrink by it: the scale is set only up to a linear transformation [Baker, 1987, p. 112]. Each calibration method fixes the scale in its own way, which its documentation states.

Calibration#

Calibration estimates the items’ parameters, with their standard errors, from a response matrix. The examinees’ abilities are unknown too, and the likelihood of the responses depends on them. The three methods irtlib implements differ in what they do with the abilities.

Joint maximum likelihood treats each examinee’s ability as one more parameter and maximizes the likelihood of the responses over the abilities and the item parameters together, with no assumption about how abilities are distributed in the population [Baker, 1987, p. 112]. Each examinee adds a parameter, so the number of parameters grows with the sample [Bock and Aitkin, 1981, p. 443]: for the Rasch model, as examinees are added to a test of fixed length, the difficulty estimates converge to limits that differ from the difficulties by a term of order \(1/M\) [Haberman, 2004, p. 13]. Haberman (p. 35) finds it “difficult to advocate use of JMLE except for preliminary estimation of parameters”. An examinee who answers every item correctly, or none, has no finite ability estimate, and an item answered correctly by every examinee or by none has no finite difficulty, so both are left out before estimation [Wright and Douglas, 1977, p. 294]. irtlib calibrates the Rasch model and the 2PL this way (irtlib.jml).

Conditional maximum likelihood applies to the Rasch model alone. There, an examinee’s number of correct responses is a sufficient statistic for its ability, so the likelihood of the responses given each examinee’s number correct holds the item difficulties alone, and “does not depend on the distribution function” of ability [Haberman, 2004, p. 35]. No such statistic eliminates the ability from the likelihood of the other models [Bock and Aitkin, 1981, p. 443]. The estimates are consistent as the number of examinees grows [Andersen, 1970, p. 286, Theorem 1] (irtlib.cml).

Marginal maximum likelihood integrates the abilities out over a population distribution and maximizes the likelihood of the item parameters alone, and Bock and Aitkin’s EM algorithm makes it feasible for any number of items, with one small problem per item in each cycle [Bock and Aitkin, 1981, pp. 444, 447]. The integral becomes a sum over the nodes of a quadrature grid [Bock and Aitkin, 1981, p. 445]. Under the 3PL, different triples \((a_i, b_i, c_i)\) can trace similar curves where the examinees are, so that the likelihood is “nearly flat along one or more dimensions” [Mislevy, 1986, p. 189]. Priors on the item parameters then make the estimates Bayes modal: they maximize the likelihood times the prior, and the EM algorithm carries over [Mislevy, 1986, p. 182]. irtlib calibrates the 1PL to the 4PL this way, with or without priors, and it is the method that takes a response matrix with missing responses (irtlib.mml).

Ability Estimation#

With the items calibrated, each examinee’s ability is estimated from its responses, with a standard error. irtlib has four estimators (irtlib.ability):

  • the maximum likelihood (ML) estimate maximizes the likelihood of the responses, and its standard error is the inverse square root of the test information [Magis and Raîche, 2012, p. 4, Equations 2 and 3]. Responses all correct give an estimate of \(+\infty\), and responses all incorrect one of \(-\infty\) [Warm, 1989, p. 442];

  • the maximum a posteriori (MAP), or Bayes modal, estimate maximizes the posterior, the likelihood times a prior density of ability [Magis and Raîche, 2012, p. 5, Equation 5];

  • the expected a posteriori (EAP) estimate is the mean of the posterior, and its standard error is the posterior standard deviation [Bock and Mislevy, 1982, p. 433];

  • Warm’s weighted likelihood (WLE) estimate removes the first-order bias of the maximum likelihood estimate [Warm, 1989, p. 427].

Checking a Calibration#

Item fit compares, item by item, the responses a calibration predicts with those observed (irtlib.fit). Orlando and Thissen’s S-X² groups examinees by their number-correct score, which is observed, rather than by an ability estimate, whose groups depend on the model [Orlando and Thissen, 2000, p. 52]; it applies to every model. The weighted and unweighted mean squares of the Rasch model compare each response with its expectation at the examinee’s estimated ability [Wright and Masters, 1982, pp. 97-99].

A model with more parameters always fits at least as well, and model comparison asks whether the improvement outweighs the parameters spent (irtlib.comparison): the likelihood-ratio test of nested models [Orlando and Thissen, 2000, p. 51], the information criteria AIC [Akaike, 1974, p. 719] and BIC [Schwarz, 1978, p. 461], and \(G^2\), which compares the model with the general multinomial over response patterns [Bock and Aitkin, 1981, p. 450]. BIC selects the true model as the sample grows when it is among the candidates, and AIC does not; AIC is efficient when no candidate is true [Vrieze, 2012, Pages 10-11].

irtlib’s Parts#

  • irtlib.ItemBank holds the items of a test, one row \([a, b, c, d]\) per item, with their names. It reads and writes them as a CSV file, and draws random banks.

  • irtlib.irt holds the item response functions and the quantities derived from them: information, the standard error, the log-likelihood and their derivatives.

  • irtlib.quadrature holds the quadrature grid that calibration and ability estimation integrate over, the ability priors, and each examinee’s posterior over the grid.

  • irtlib.mml, irtlib.cml and irtlib.jml hold the calibrators. Each returns the same result, a Calibration (ADR-0006: The Calibrator Contract).

  • irtlib.ability holds the ability estimators, irtlib.fit item and person fit, and irtlib.comparison model comparison.

  • irtlib.drawing draws responses from the model, for simulation and parameter-recovery studies. Every function that draws random numbers takes them from its rng argument (ADR-0005: Every Draw Comes From a Generator the Caller Passes).
