Item Fit – irtlib.fit#

Item fit: Orlando and Thissen’s S-X² statistic for every model, and the mean squares of the Rasch model.

Item fit compares, item by item, the responses a calibration predicts with those observed.

S-X². Orlando and Thissen group examinees by their number-correct score, which is observed, rather than by an ability estimate, whose groups depend on the model [Orlando and Thissen, 2000] (p. 52). For item \(i\) of \(M\) and score \(k = 1, \dots, M - 1\), with \(N_k\) examinees at score \(k\) and \(O_{ik}\) the observed proportion of them answering item \(i\) correctly,

\[S\text{-}X^2_i = \sum_{k=1}^{M-1} N_k \frac{(O_{ik} - E_{ik})^2}{E_{ik}(1 - E_{ik})}\]

(Orlando and Thissen [2000], p. 54, Equation 13). The expected proportion is

\[E_{ik} = \frac{\sum_q P_i(X_q)\, S^{*i}_{k-1}(X_q)\, A_q}{\sum_q S_k(X_q)\, A_q}\]

(p. 54, Equation 12), where \(S_k(X_q)\) is the likelihood of a score of \(k\) at ability \(X_q\) and \(S^{*i}_k\) the same without item \(i\), both from Lord and Wingersky’s recursion over items (p. 53, Equations 6-11): adding an item with probability \(T\) turns \(S^*_k\) into \(T S^*_{k-1} + (1 - T) S^*_k\). The nodes and their normal masses \(A_q\) are the calibration’s grid.

The two cells of each score row, incorrect and correct, are collapsed as mirt 1.48 collapses them (source R/utils.R:2410-2546): rows no examinee reached are dropped, a row with one examinee is merged into the next, the last into the one before, and while an expected count is below min_expected, the first such row is merged into its neighbour, the first row into the second, the last into the one before and any other into the neighbour with fewer examinees. Orlando and Thissen (p. 56) collapse from both ends toward the middle until every expected count is at least 1. The statistic sums \((O - E)^2 / E\) over the cells left, and its degrees of freedom are the rows left less the number of parameters estimated for the item (p. 56), floored at 0. An item whose collapsed table leaves no degrees of freedom, including one collapsed to a single row, which mirt skips, has none and no statistic.

S-X² “cannot handle incomplete data directly” [Zhang and Wang, 2022] (p. 705), so it needs complete responses (ADR-0006).

Mean squares. The weighted and unweighted mean squares compare each response with its expectation at the examinee’s estimated ability (Wright and Masters [1982], pp. 97-99). Wright and Masters define them for the models of their book, all built from the Rasch model (p. 10; p. 100, Table 5.4a), whose model for dichotomous responses (p. 38, Equation 3.1.1) is the 1PL, every item with the same slope \(a\), with person parameters \(a\theta_j\) and item parameters \(ab_i\). For examinee \(j\) and item \(i\), with \(P_{ij}\) the probability of a correct response at the examinee’s ability and \(Q_{ij} = 1 - P_{ij}\), the score residual \(y_{ij} = u_{ij} - P_{ij}\) (p. 98, Equation 5.4.2) has variance \(W_{ij}\) and kurtosis \(C_{ij}\) (p. 98, Equations 5.4.3 and 5.4.4), which for the two categories of a dichotomous item are

\[W_{ij} = P_{ij} Q_{ij}, \quad C_{ij} = P_{ij} Q_{ij} (P_{ij}^3 + Q_{ij}^3),\]

and the standardized residual is \(z_{ij} = y_{ij} / W_{ij}^{1/2}\) (p. 99, Equation 5.4.5). Over the \(N\) examinees, the unweighted and the weighted mean squares, which Wright and Masters write \(u_i\) and \(v_i\), are

\[U_i = \frac{1}{N} \sum_j z_{ij}^2, \quad V_i = \frac{\sum_j W_{ij} z_{ij}^2}{\sum_j W_{ij}} = \frac{\sum_j y_{ij}^2}{\sum_j W_{ij}}\]

(p. 99, Equations 5.4.6 and 5.4.7). irtlib calls \(U_i\) the outfit and \(V_i\) the infit, as mirt 1.48 does (source R/itemfit.R:584, 588). \(U_i\) is sensitive to unexpected responses of examinees for whom the item is far too easy or far too hard, and the weights \(W_{ij}\), smallest for those examinees, lessen their influence on \(V_i\) (pp. 99, 101). When the data fit the model both have expectation 1, \(U_i\) with variance \(\sum_j (C_{ij} / W_{ij}^2) / N^2 - 1 / N\) (p. 100, Table 5.4a) and \(V_i\) with variance

\[q_i^2 = \frac{\sum_j (C_{ij} - W_{ij}^2)}{\left(\sum_j W_{ij}\right)^2}\]

(p. 101, Equation 5.4.8). The weighted mean square is standardized to

\[t_i = (V_i^{1/3} - 1)(3 / q_i) + q_i / 3,\]

which has a mean near 0 and a standard deviation near 1 when the data fit (p. 101; p. 100, Table 5.4a). Wright and Masters estimate the person and item parameters from the same data as the residuals, which lowers the expected mean square by the factor \((M - 1)(N - 1) / (MN)\) for \(M\) items (p. 101).

The abilities are the EAP estimates of irtlib.ability.expected_a_posteriori() on the calibration’s grid, abilities normal with mean 0 and variance 1 (ADR-0006). mirt 1.48 takes EAP estimates by default too (R/itemfit.R:268, 535-538), on the grid of its fscores. irtlib follows mirt in three places that no source on the shelf covers: it standardizes \(U_i\) in the same way as \(V_i\), with the standard deviation from Table 5.4a in place of \(q_i\) (R/itemfit.R:585, 587); it raises a variance \(W_{ij}\) whose square is below \(10^{-5}\) to \(10^{-5/2}\), leaving \(C_{ij}\) and \(y_{ij}\) as they are (R/personfit.R:189-200), so that a mean square’s variance can fall below 0, and its \(t\) is then NaN; and it caps the standard deviation of either mean square at 1.4142 before standardizing (R/itemfit.R:586, 591). mirt computes the mean squares of any model from its probabilities (R/personfit.R:181-194), and with missing responses sums over the examinees who answered each item, with \(N\) their number (R/itemfit.R:577-584). Wright and Masters’ sums run over every examinee, with no rule for a missing response, so irtlib’s mean squares need complete responses (ADR-0006), and a calibration of the 1PL by marginal maximum likelihood, whose grid the abilities are estimated on.

class irtlib.fit.MeanSquares(outfit: NDArray[floating[Any]], outfit_t: NDArray[floating[Any]], infit: NDArray[floating[Any]], infit_t: NDArray[floating[Any]])[source]#

The outfit and infit mean squares of each item, each with its standardized value.

Both mean squares have expectation 1 when the responses fit the model, and less when the abilities are estimated from the same responses, as they are here.

Parameters:
outfitnumpy.ndarray

The unweighted mean square of each item, Wright and Masters’ \(u_i\): the mean, over examinees, of the squared standardized residuals.

outfit_tnumpy.ndarray

The outfit standardized by the same cube-root transformation as infit_t, with the outfit’s own standard deviation under the model; NaN where its variance under the model is not positive.

infitnumpy.ndarray

The weighted mean square of each item, Wright and Masters’ \(v_i\): the squared residuals summed over examinees, over the sum of their variances.

infit_tnumpy.ndarray

The infit standardized by a cube-root transformation, with its standard deviation under the model; NaN where its variance under the model is not positive.

irtlib.fit.mean_squares(calibration: Calibration, responses: NDArray[floating[Any]]) → MeanSquares[source]#

Return the outfit and infit mean squares of every item of a 1PL calibration, from its responses.

Each response is compared with its probability at the examinee’s EAP ability estimate on the calibration’s grid, with abilities normal with mean 0 and variance 1.

Parameters:
calibrationCalibration

A calibration of the 1PL, whose items all have the same slope, c = 0 and d = 1, of the marginal likelihood, on whose grid the abilities are estimated.

responsesnumpy.ndarray

The complete response matrix the calibration estimated its items from: one row per examinee, one column per item, 1 or 0.

Returns:
MeanSquares

Each item’s outfit and infit, and their standardized values.

Raises:
TypeError

If the responses are neither Booleans, integers nor floats.

ValueError

If the calibration’s items are not those of a 1PL, or its likelihood is not marginal, so that it has no grid; or the responses fail irtlib.calibration.validate_responses(), have a missing response, or do not have one column per item of the calibration.

Examples

>>> import numpy
>>> from irtlib.drawing import draw_responses
>>> from irtlib.mml import MMLCalibrator
>>> items = numpy.column_stack([numpy.ones(4), [-1.0, -0.3, 0.3, 1.0], numpy.zeros(4), numpy.ones(4)])
>>> responses = draw_responses(numpy.random.default_rng(1).normal(size=500), items, rng=2)
>>> fit = mean_squares(MMLCalibrator("1PL").calibrate(responses), responses)
>>> fit.outfit.round(3), fit.infit.round(3)
(array([0.751, 0.772, 0.741, 0.723]), array([0.821, 0.807, 0.777, 0.791]))
irtlib.fit.s_x2(calibration: Calibration, responses: NDArray[floating[Any]], *, min_expected: float = 1.0) → ItemFit[source]#

Return the S-X² item-fit statistic of every item of a calibration, from the responses it was calibrated on.

Every item is evaluated at its estimates, including the items the calibration’s bank leaves out.

Parameters:
calibrationCalibration

The calibration whose items are evaluated, of the marginal likelihood, whose grid the expected counts are integrated over.

responsesnumpy.ndarray

The complete response matrix the calibration estimated its items from: one row per examinee, one column per item, 1 or 0.

min_expectedfloat, optional

The smallest expected count a cell may keep after collapsing, at least 0. Default is 1. The expected counts are integrated over the calibration’s grid.

Returns:
ItemFit

Each item’s statistic, degrees of freedom and p-value.

Raises:
TypeError

If the responses are neither Booleans, integers nor floats.

ValueError

If the responses fail irtlib.calibration.validate_responses(), have a missing response, or do not have one column per item of the calibration; min_expected is negative; or the calibration’s likelihood is not marginal, so that it has no grid.
