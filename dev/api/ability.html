Ability Estimation – irtlib.ability#

Ability estimation: each examinee’s ability, with its standard error, from responses to items of known parameters.

Each estimator takes a response matrix with one row per examinee and one column per item, holding 1, 0 or NaN where the examinee did not answer the item, and the items, one row [a, b, c, d] per column. It returns an AbilityEstimates: for each examinee an estimate, its standard error and the number of evaluations of the estimator’s objective or of its derivatives. An examinee’s estimate depends only on the items it answered, so its row gives the same estimate alone as in any matrix: exactly where it answered every item, and otherwise to the rounding of a sum over more terms, which can also move a search’s last step and change its evaluation count by one, since the maximum likelihood and weighted likelihood searches, and MAP’s under a normal prior, search every row of a matrix at once with the arithmetic of one row. Below, \(L_j(\theta)\) is the likelihood of examinee \(j\)’s responses \(u_{ij}\), the product of \(P_i^{u_{ij}} Q_i^{1 - u_{ij}}\) over the items \(i\) it answered, and \(I(\theta)\) the test information of those items, both as irtlib.irt defines them.

Maximum likelihood. The estimate maximizes \(L_j(\theta)\), and its standard error is \(1 / \sqrt{I(\hat\theta)}\) (Magis and Raîche [2012], p. 4, Equations 2 and 3). The likelihood of responses all correct rises with the ability and that of responses all incorrect falls, so their estimates are \(+\infty\) and \(-\infty\) (Warm [1989], p. 442). As \(\theta \to -\infty\) each \(P_i\) tends to \(c_i\), and as \(\theta \to +\infty\) to \(d_i\), so the likelihood tends to \(\prod_i c_i^{u_{ij}} (1 - c_i)^{1 - u_{ij}}\) and to \(\prod_i d_i^{u_{ij}} (1 - d_i)^{1 - u_{ij}}\): under the 3PL and 4PL, responses that are neither all correct nor all incorrect can also have their supremum at an infinity. The maximum likelihood estimate is the maximizer over the extended real line: \(+\infty\) or \(-\infty\) wherever the likelihood comes closest to its supremum only in that limit, with standard error \(\infty\), since the information tends to 0 at either infinity. An examinee who answered no item has a constant likelihood, which every ability maximizes: its estimate is NaN, and its standard error \(\infty\), the information being 0. The caller has two ways to finite numbers, never used together:

  • bounds: the estimate maximizes the likelihood within them, and responses all correct or all incorrect give the bound they point to. Bounds are always set in practice (Warm [1989], p. 442), and catR 3.17 sets them by default: its default constantPatt = NULL estimates such responses by maximum likelihood too (Magis et al. [2022], p. 115), which returns the end of its range that the score points to (source R/thetaEst.R:9-10, 100-109).

  • fallback: another estimator, called once on the rows whose estimate is not finite, the unanswered rows included, whose estimates, standard errors and evaluation counts replace theirs; the from_fallback field of AbilityEstimates marks those rows.

Where an item answered has \(c_i > 0\) or \(d_i < 1\), the likelihood can have several maxima, and the search evaluates the log-likelihood at equally spaced abilities of the range: 101 when no item answered has a slope \(a_i\) above 2.5, 0.12 apart on [-6, 6], and otherwise \(\lceil 40 \max_i a_i \rceil + 1\), 0.3 divided by the steepest slope apart on [-6, 6], since a steep item can make a peak narrower than 0.12, up to 2,401 points, 0.005 apart, for slopes of 60 and above, so that a pathological slope cannot exhaust memory. It refines the best between its neighbours by Newton’s method on the log-likelihood’s first and second derivatives, from the vertex of the parabola through the three points, keeping each step inside a bracket that shrinks to the side the first derivative points to and bisecting the bracket where a step would leave it, and it keeps the refinement only if no worse: the search of the MAP estimate below. When every item answered has \(c_i = 0\) and \(d_i = 1\), the second derivative is \(-I\) (see the MAP estimate below), so the log-likelihood is concave and has at most one maximum. The search then skips the points: the estimate is a bound where the first derivative points out of the range, and otherwise the zero of the first derivative, found by the same bracketed Newton’s method from the middle of the range. On about 38,000 random mixed patterns of 1 to 60 3PL and 4PL items with slopes from 0.5 to 2.5, half with responses drawn from the model, 101 and 201 points found the global maximum on [-6, 6] every time and 81 points missed it once, while Brent’s method over the whole range, with no points, missed it 203 times, by a median of 3.7 in ability. On about 27,000 patterns of 1 to 30 items, half of them with slopes up to 60, the points spaced by the steepest slope missed it twice, each time by less than 0.001 in log-likelihood, where 101 points missed it 50 times and 201 points 11 (measured 2026-11-28). Without bounds the range starts as [-6, 6]. Beyond the lower end of the range, a correct response’s probability is at most its value at the end, since it rises with the ability, and an incorrect one’s at most \(1 - c_i\); beyond the upper end, a correct one’s is at most \(d_i\) and an incorrect one’s at most its value at the end. While these bounds leave room beyond an end for a log-likelihood above both the best found and the two limits, that end moves out, twice as far each time, and the new stretch is searched the same way, with as many points as the range, so that their spacing doubles with each stretch. The estimate is then the infinity of the larger limit, \(-\infty\) of two equal ones, if that limit is at least the best value found, within a relative \(10^{-12}\), and the best ability found otherwise. After 64 moves of an end, which only items of a vanishing discrimination need, the search stops at the best ability found and warns.

mirt 1.48 also gives \(+\infty\) or \(-\infty\) to responses all correct or all incorrect (R/fscores.internal.R:397-405), with no standard error, NA, where irtlib’s is \(\infty\), and NA to both for an examinee who answered no item (R/fscores.internal.R:94-96, 185). It searches the other responses with R’s nlm from the EAP estimate, up to an ability of \(\pm 20\) (R/fscores.R:305; R/fscores.internal.R:687), takes the standard error from nlm’s numerical Hessian, the observed information (R/fscores.internal.R:1072-1101), which is \(I\) for items with \(c_i = 0\) and \(d_i = 1\) (see the MAP estimate below), and turns an estimate whose standard error exceeds 30 into an infinity (R/fscores.internal.R:1094-1096), which irtlib does not do.

Maximum a posteriori. The MAP, or Bayes modal, estimate maximizes the posterior, \(\log \pi(\theta) + \log L_j(\theta)\) for a prior density \(\pi\) (Magis and Raîche [2012], p. 5, Equation 5; Warm [1989], p. 430). For a normal prior of variance \(\sigma^2\) its standard error is \(1 / \sqrt{1 / \sigma^2 + I(\hat\theta)}\) (Magis and Raîche [2012], p. 5, Equation 6). For any prior, irtlib takes \(1 / \sqrt{I(\hat\theta) - (\log\pi)''(\hat\theta)}\), with the prior’s second derivative a central difference of step \(10^{-4}\): Equation 6 for a normal prior, and for a uniform one, inside its support, the maximum likelihood standard error, as catR 3.17 computes them (R/semTheta.R:43-50, 82-89). Where the difference is not finite, at the edge of a bounded prior’s support, the prior adds nothing; where the sum is not positive, the standard error is \(\infty\). The search evaluates the log-posterior at equally spaced abilities within the bounds: under a normal prior whose precision \(1 / \sigma^2\) is a finite positive float, as many as the maximum likelihood search for the same items, and under any other prior 201, 0.06 apart on [-6, 6]. Three or more tied at the maximum make a flat posterior, and the estimate is the middle one; otherwise the best is refined between its neighbours, and the refinement is kept only if no worse, so the estimate stays inside the prior’s support and is the highest of the modes the points separate. With a normal prior \(N(\mu, \sigma^2)\) the refinement is the maximum likelihood search’s Newton’s method, with \(-(\theta - \mu) / \sigma^2\) and \(-1 / \sigma^2\) added to the derivatives, and on items that all have \(c_i = 0\) and \(d_i = 1\) the log-posterior is concave too, and the search skips the points as the maximum likelihood search does. With any other prior the refinement is SciPy’s bounded search, scipy.optimize.minimize_scalar. An examinee who answered no item gets the prior’s mode.

mirt 1.48 maximizes the same posterior with nlm and takes the standard error from nlm’s numerical Hessian (R/fscores.internal.R:684-703, 1047-1070): the observed information plus the prior’s precision. When every answered item has \(c_i = 0\) and \(d_i = 1\), the observed information is \(I\) exactly (Warm [1989], p. 449, whose model fixes \(d_i = 1\), p. 428, Equation 1), and the two standard errors agree.

Expected a posteriori. The EAP estimate is the mean of the examinee’s posterior over the grid of irtlib.quadrature, and its standard error the posterior standard deviation (Bock and Mislevy [1982], p. 433, Equations 4 and 5; Magis and Raîche [2012], pp. 5-6, Equations 10 and 11). It is finite whatever the responses, and an examinee who answered no item gets the prior’s mean and standard deviation on the grid. irtlib’s default grid and prior are mirt 1.48’s: 121 nodes on [-6, 6] (R/fscores.R:302, 318-319; R/fscores.internal.R:231) with the standard normal density at the nodes, normalized (R/fscores.internal.R:316-317). mirt gives NA to an examinee who answered no item.

Weighted likelihood. Warm’s estimate is the root of the modified score equation

\[\sum_i \frac{(u_{ij} - P_i) P_i'}{P_i Q_i} + \frac{J}{2 I} = 0, \quad J = \sum_i \frac{P_i' P_i''}{P_i Q_i}\]

(Warm [1989], p. 431, Equation 10; p. 430), the derivative of the log of the weighted likelihood \(w(\theta) L_j(\theta)\), with \(\partial \log w / \partial\theta = J / (2 I)\), which the estimate maximizes (p. 446, Equations A3 and A4). It removes the first-order bias of the maximum likelihood estimate (p. 427; p. 431, Theorem). When every \(c_i = 0\), \(w = I^{1/2}\) (p. 431), in Warm’s model, which fixes \(d_i = 1\) (p. 428, Equation 1). The estimate’s variance is asymptotically \(1 / I\), that of the maximum likelihood estimate (p. 431), and its standard error is \(1 / \sqrt{I(\hat\theta)}\), catR 3.17’s default (R/semTheta.R:90-96); Magis and Raîche [2012] (p. 6, Equation 14) give a corrected one, which irtlib does not use. The search evaluates the modified score at 121 equally spaced abilities within the bounds. Each change of sign from positive to negative, refined by Brent’s method step for step as SciPy 1.18.1’s scipy.optimize.brentq takes it (source scipy/optimize/Zeros/brentq.c:36-130), from the values of the scan at its ends, is a maximum of the weighted likelihood, and so is a bound the score points out of. Of several, the estimate is the one of largest weighted likelihood, ranked by integrating the score from the lower bound with scipy.integrate.quad. Warm finds finite estimates for responses all correct or all incorrect on his tests (p. 443, Table 1), and Magis and Raîche [2012] (p. 11) propose the estimator for them; irtlib’s estimate lies within the bounds whatever the responses. An examinee who answered no item has no estimate, NaN, and standard error \(\infty\), as under maximum likelihood.

mirt 1.48 maximizes \(\log L_j + \tfrac12 \log I\) instead (R/fscores.internal.R:705-738), Warm’s estimate when every answered item has \(c_i = 0\) and \(d_i = 1\), and takes its standard error from nlm’s numerical Hessian of that weighted log-likelihood (R/fscores.internal.R:1103-1119).

irtlib.ability.expected_a_posteriori(responses: ArrayLike, items: NDArray[floating[Any]], *, grid: QuadratureGrid | None = None, log_prior: LogPrior | None = None, grid_prior: GridPrior | None = None) → AbilityEstimates[source]#

Return each examinee’s EAP estimate, the mean of its posterior over a grid, with the posterior standard deviation.

Every estimate is finite, whatever the responses; an examinee who answered no item gets the prior’s mean and standard deviation on the grid. The evaluation count is the number of grid nodes.

Parameters:
responsesarray_like

The response matrix, one row per examinee and one column per item: 1 or True for a correct response, 0 or False for an incorrect one, and NaN for a missing one.

itemsnumpy.ndarray

The items [a, b, c, d], a matrix of shape (n, 4), one row per column of responses, with a > 0 and 0 <= c < d <= 1.

gridQuadratureGrid or None, optional

The nodes the posterior is computed on. Default is QuadratureGrid.uniform(), 121 nodes on [-6, 6].

log_priorLogPrior or None, optional

The prior’s log-density. Default is normal_log_prior(), the standard normal.

grid_priorGridPrior or None, optional

A grid and prior evaluated once, in place of grid and log_prior, for a caller that estimates with the same ones many times. Default is None.

Returns:
AbilityEstimates

The posterior means, the posterior standard deviations and the grid’s size, one of each per examinee.

Raises:
TypeError

If the responses are neither Booleans, integers nor floats.

ValueError

If grid_prior is given together with grid or log_prior. Also if items is not an (n, 4) matrix, responses is not a matrix with one column per item, a response is not 0, 1 or NaN, or the log-prior is NaN or +inf at a node or has no mass on the grid.

Examples

>>> import numpy
>>> items = numpy.array([[1.0, -1.0, 0.0, 1.0], [1.5, 0.0, 0.2, 1.0], [0.8, 1.0, 0.0, 0.95]])
>>> estimates = expected_a_posteriori(numpy.array([[1, 0, numpy.nan], [1, 1, 1]]), items)
>>> estimates.theta.round(4), estimates.standard_errors.round(4)
(array([-0.2809,  0.8555]), array([0.7837, 0.828 ]))

irtlib.ability.maximum_likelihood(responses: ArrayLike, items: NDArray[floating[Any]], *, bounds: tuple[float, float] | None = None, fallback: Callable[[NDArray[floating[Any]], NDArray[floating[Any]]], AbilityEstimates] | None = None, tol: float = 1e-06) → AbilityEstimates[source]#

Return each examinee’s maximum likelihood estimate, with its standard error.

Without bounds the estimate maximizes the likelihood over the whole ability scale, infinities included: it is inf or -inf where the likelihood comes closest to its supremum only in that limit, as it does for responses all correct or all incorrect, and its standard error is then inf. With bounds it maximizes the likelihood within them, and is finite. An examinee who answered no item has no estimate, NaN, and standard error inf. A fallback replaces every estimate that is not finite, infinite or NaN.

Parameters:
responsesarray_like

The response matrix, one row per examinee and one column per item: 1 or True for a correct response, 0 or False for an incorrect one, and NaN for a missing one.

itemsnumpy.ndarray

The items [a, b, c, d], a matrix of shape (n, 4), one row per column of responses, with a > 0 and 0 <= c < d <= 1.

boundstuple[float, float] or None, optional

The range the estimate is searched in; responses all correct or all incorrect give the bound they point to. Default is None, the whole ability scale.

fallbackCallable or None, optional

An estimator called as fallback(responses, items), once, on the rows whose estimate is not finite, such as functools.partial(expected_a_posteriori, log_prior=normal_log_prior(0.0, 1.0)); its estimates, standard errors and evaluation counts replace theirs, and from_fallback marks them. Default is None, which keeps them.

tolfloat, optional

The absolute tolerance of the search, in the ability scale. Default is 1e-6.

Returns:
AbilityEstimates

The estimates, their standard errors, and the number of evaluations of each examinee’s likelihood or of its derivatives.

Raises:
TypeError

If the responses are neither Booleans, integers nor floats.

ValueError

If both bounds and fallback are given, bounds is not a finite interval, tol is not positive, items is not an (n, 4) matrix, responses is not a matrix with one column per item, a response is not 0, 1 or NaN, or the fallback does not return one estimate per row it is given.

Examples

>>> import numpy
>>> items = numpy.array([[1.0, -1.0, 0.0, 1.0], [1.5, 0.0, 0.2, 1.0], [0.8, 1.0, 0.0, 0.95]])
>>> responses = numpy.array([[1, 0, numpy.nan], [1, 1, 1]])
>>> maximum_likelihood(responses, items).theta.round(4)
array([-0.6463,     inf])
>>> maximum_likelihood(responses, items, bounds=(-4.0, 4.0)).theta.round(4)
array([-0.6463,  4.    ])
>>> estimates = maximum_likelihood(responses, items, fallback=expected_a_posteriori)
>>> estimates.theta.round(4), estimates.from_fallback
(array([-0.6463,  0.8555]), array([False,  True]))
irtlib.ability.weighted_likelihood(responses: ArrayLike, items: NDArray[floating[Any]], *, bounds: tuple[float, float] = (-6.0, 6.0), tol: float = 1e-06) → AbilityEstimates[source]#

Return each examinee’s weighted likelihood estimate, Warm’s, with its standard error.

The estimate is the root of Warm’s modified score within the bounds; of several, the one of largest weighted likelihood, and of none, the bound the score points to. An examinee who answered no item has no estimate, NaN, and standard error inf. The evaluation count is the number of modified-score evaluations. A ranking integral that does not converge warns with scipy.integrate.IntegrationWarning.

Parameters:
responsesarray_like

The response matrix, one row per examinee and one column per item: 1 or True for a correct response, 0 or False for an incorrect one, and NaN for a missing one.

itemsnumpy.ndarray

The items [a, b, c, d], a matrix of shape (n, 4), one row per column of responses, with a > 0 and 0 <= c < d <= 1.

boundstuple[float, float], optional

The range the estimate is searched in. Default is (THETA_MIN_EXTENDED, THETA_MAX_EXTENDED), [-6, 6].

tolfloat, optional

The absolute tolerance of the root search, in the ability scale. Default is 1e-6.

Returns:
AbilityEstimates

The estimates, their standard errors, inf where the information is 0, and the evaluation counts.

Raises:
TypeError

If the responses are neither Booleans, integers nor floats.

ValueError

If bounds is not a finite interval, tol is not positive, items is not an (n, 4) matrix, responses is not a matrix with one column per item, or a response is not 0, 1 or NaN.

Examples

>>> import numpy
>>> items = numpy.array([[1.0, -1.0, 0.0, 1.0], [1.5, 0.0, 0.2, 1.0], [0.8, 1.0, 0.0, 0.95]])
>>> estimates = weighted_likelihood(numpy.array([[1, 0, numpy.nan], [1, 1, 1]]), items)
>>> estimates.theta.round(4), estimates.standard_errors.round(4)
