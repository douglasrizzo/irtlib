Joint Maximum Likelihood#

API:

irtlib.jml.JMLCalibrator

Sources:

Wright and Douglas [1977]; Baker [1987]; Haberman [2004]; Mislevy and Stocking [1989]

Motivation#

Joint maximum likelihood (JML) treats every examinee’s ability as a parameter, and maximizes the likelihood of the responses over the abilities and the item parameters together, with no assumption about the distribution of ability [Baker, 1987, p. 112]. As each examinee adds a parameter, the item estimates do not converge to the item parameters as examinees are added to a test of fixed length [Bock and Aitkin, 1981, p. 443]; [Baker, 1987, pp. 117-118]: for the Rasch model they converge to limits that differ from the parameters by a term of order \(1/M\) [Haberman, 2004, p. 13], and Haberman (p. 35) finds it “difficult to advocate use of JMLE except for preliminary estimation of parameters”.

irtlib has it for comparing calibration methods and for preliminary estimates, for the Rasch model and the 2PL. It returns the examinees’ abilities with the items. Conditional Maximum Likelihood gives consistent Rasch difficulties, and Marginal Maximum Likelihood calibrates every model.

Definition#

With \(N\) examinees and \(M\) items, the logarithm of the joint likelihood is

\[\sum_{j=1}^N \sum_{i=1}^M \left[u_{ij} \log P_i(\theta_j) + (1 - u_{ij}) \log Q_i(\theta_j)\right]\]

[Baker, 1987, p. 112, Equations 1 and 3], for the 2PL (p. 114, Equation 5) or for the Rasch model, whose slopes are 1 (p. 115, Equation 9; Wright and Douglas [1977, p. 282, Equations 1-3]).

Each cycle has two steps, after Birnbaum’s paradigm [Baker, 1987, p. 112]:

  • the item step maximizes, for each item, its grouped log-likelihood with the abilities fixed;

  • the ability step estimates each examinee’s ability with the items fixed, by maximum likelihood.

The likelihood is the same for abilities and items moved together along a linear transformation of the ability scale [Baker, 1987, p. 112], so the scale has to be fixed.

Implementation#

Examinees and items left out. An examinee with no item or every item correct has no finite ability estimate, and an item answered correctly by every examinee or by none no finite estimate either [Wright and Douglas, 1977, p. 294]; [Haberman, 2004, p. 7]. Both are left out before estimation, the examinees first and then the items, repeatedly until neither is left, as Wright and Douglas’s editing does (p. 294), and the items left out get no estimate. For the Rasch model, the joint likelihood of the responses left can still lack a maximum: it has none exactly when the examinees with at most some number correct answered none of a group of items and the others all of the rest [Haberman, 2004, p. 11, Theorem 1], the same split that leaves the conditional likelihood of Conditional Maximum Likelihood none once those examinees and items are left out, and calibration raises an error naming the groups.

The item step maximizes each item’s grouped log-likelihood by the Fisher scoring of Marginal Maximum Likelihood’s M-step, with the estimated abilities in place of the quadrature nodes and the observed counts in place of the expected ones. For the Rasch model and the 2PL the Hessian is the information, so the steps are Newton-Raphson’s [Baker, 1987, p. 116], those of Wright and Douglas (p. 284, Equation 11) for the difficulties and of Baker (p. 114, Equation 8) for the 2PL’s intercept and slope.

The ability step uses irtlib.ability.maximum_likelihood(). Under the Rasch model every examinee with the same number correct has the same estimate [Wright and Douglas, 1977, p. 283]; [Baker, 1987, p. 115], and under either model every examinee with the same responses, so each is estimated once.

The scale. After each ability step, abilities and items are moved to fix the scale (ADR-0008): for the Rasch model so that the difficulties have mean 0, as BICAL fixes it [Baker, 1987, p. 115] and as Wright and Douglas recentre them [Wright and Douglas, 1977, p. 283]; [Wright and Douglas, 1977, p. 284, step iv]; for the 2PL so that the abilities of the examinees kept have mean 0 and standard deviation 1, its divisor the number of examinees, as LOGIST fixes it [Baker, 1987, p. 112]. LOGIST standardizes only the abilities between -3 and 3 [Mislevy and Stocking, 1989, p. 60], where irtlib standardizes every examinee kept.

Starting values and stopping. The cycles start from Wright and Douglas’s starting values [Wright and Douglas, 1977, p. 283, Equation 9]; [Wright and Douglas, 1977, p. 284, Equation 10], the abilities \(\log(r / (M - r))\) for \(r\) correct and the difficulties \(\log((N - s_i) / s_i)\) centred, for \(s_i\) correct responses to item \(i\); for the 2PL, which they do not cover, the slopes start at 1 and the abilities are standardized. A calibration stops when no item parameter changes by more than its tolerance in a cycle, the rule and the defaults of Marginal Maximum Likelihood (ADR-0007). Wright and Douglas stop when the mean squared change of the difficulties falls below \(10^{-4}\) [Wright and Douglas, 1977, p. 285, Equation 15], which stopped 0.0007 and 0.0009 short of the maximum on 20 items and on LSAT-6, where irtlib’s rule stops within \(2 \times 10^{-7}\) of it (measured 2026-11-27). The ability step compares likelihoods, which can leave changes of about \(10^{-8}\) from cycle to cycle, as on a 2PL of 20 items and 400 examinees, so a tolerance below that may not be met (measured 2026-11-28).

The Rasch model’s bias and its correction. Wright and Douglas write each difficulty estimate as the conditional one plus a bias, \(d_i^* = d_i + a_{ri}\) at score \(r\), approximating the bias by the change in the conditional ability for score \(r\) when item \(i\) is left out, and its relative bias as \(c_{ri} = a_{ri} / d_i\) [Wright and Douglas, 1977, p. 286, Equations 25-27]. Averaged over scores and items with \(|d_i| \geq 0.5\), they find it close to \(1 / (M - 1)\) whatever the spread of the difficulties (p. 287, Equations 31-34, Table 1), so the estimates spread outward from the conditional ones, and multiplying the centred difficulties by the correction factor \((M - 1) / M\) (p. 287, Equation 35) leaves a residual bias of at most 0.012 for 20 items (p. 288, Table 2). The factor is the one “used in most versions of UCON”, and with two items, for which the joint estimates are exactly twice the conditional ones, it is exact [Wright and Douglas, 1977, p. 288]; [Haberman, 2004, pp. 17-18]. It assumes item difficulties and examinees spread “more or less uniform[ly]” over the scores [Wright and Douglas, 1977, p. 288], and it brings the estimates close to the conditional ones, which “is not the same as” removing their bias from the parameters [Baker, 1987, p. 118]. Computed exactly for 20 items equally spaced, with as many examinees at each score, the relative bias is 0.055 for difficulties within \(\pm 1\), as Wright and Douglas find, but 0.060 within \(\pm 2\) and 0.071 within \(\pm 3\), and the largest residual after the correction 0.004, 0.029 and 0.090 (measured 2026-11-27): the correction leaves more of the bias of the items far from the centre of a wide test. It is applied once the cycles stop, by default for the Rasch model; Wright and Douglas derive it for the Rasch model alone.

Standard errors. Each item’s standard errors are the square roots of the diagonal of the inverse of its item step’s information at the last cycle [Baker, 1987, p. 119], for the Rasch model \((\sum_r n_r p_{ri} (1 - p_{ri}))^{-1/2}\), with \(n_r\) examinees of score \(r\) and \(p_{ri}\) their probability of a correct response to item \(i\) [Wright and Douglas, 1977, p. 285, Equation 16]; the 2PL’s difficulty takes its standard error by the delta method, as in Marginal Maximum Likelihood. They treat the abilities as known, and with the correction they are those of the estimates before it, unless correct_errors multiplies them by its factor too. For a fixed number of items, JML’s estimates are not centred on the parameters and their asymptotic covariance is not the customary one, so “ordinary asymptotic confidence intervals” for the difficulties cannot be built from them [Haberman, 2004, pp. 26-27, Theorem 7]; [Haberman, 2004, p. 29].

The 2PL’s slopes. Under the 2PL a slope can grow without bound, as large slopes and the abilities of the examinees who answer those items correctly feed each other from cycle to cycle, the Heywood case; LOGIST imposes upper limits on the slopes [Baker, 1987, p. 113], and “the maximizing values for item discriminations under the 2PL … are sometimes infinite” [Mislevy and Stocking, 1989, p. 62]. irtlib imposes no limit. An item whose correct responses the abilities all put at or above its incorrect ones, or at or below, discriminates perfectly, and its slope has no finite estimate [Baker, 1987, p. 117], so the cycles stop before such an item step; they stop too at an item step that gives a slope that is not positive, since the ability step needs positive slopes. Either way the result holds the last complete cycle and says so. On short tests this is the usual outcome. On LSAT-6 and LSAT-7, five items and 1,000 examinees, the cycles stopped after 3 and 2 at an item step that gave one slope a value that was not positive and another 3.53 and 7.21. On random 2PL tests, slopes uniform on \([0.7, 2]\), difficulties on \([-1.5, 1.5]\) and abilities normal, the cycles stopped on all 50 tests of 8 items and 300 examinees (38 at a separation, 12 at a slope that was not positive), on 19 of 20 of 10 items and 500 examinees, 13 of 20 of 15 and 500, and 3 of 20 of 20 and 500, and on none of 10 tests each of 20 and 30 items with 1,000 examinees (measured 2026-11-28). The authors of LOGIST advise at least 20 items per examinee and 800 to 1,000 examinees per item [Mislevy and Stocking, 1989, p. 61].

The abilities. The result holds every examinee’s ability (ADR-0006), from the cycle its item estimates come from and before any correction, so that the abilities, the uncorrected item estimates and the log-likelihood go together; each ability’s standard error is that of maximum likelihood, the inverse square root of the test information, there. An examinee left out has the ability \(\infty\) or \(-\infty\), as maximum likelihood gives responses all right or all wrong.

Complete responses. JML needs complete responses, as its sources write it, an examinee’s number correct out of every item [Wright and Douglas, 1977, p. 282], and MMLCalibrator calibrates incomplete ones (ADR-0006).

Against conditional maximum likelihood. On two SAT tests of 78 and 60 items, the largest difference between the joint and conditional estimates of the Rasch difficulties was 0.097 and 0.126, against conditional standard errors of at most 0.008 [Haberman, 2004, pp. 44-45]. In Robitzsch [2021]’s simulations, JML with the correction and extreme scores removed (p. 816) had a mean absolute bias below 0.025 (p. 821) in 54.2% of the conditions, against 100% for conditional maximum likelihood (p. 823, Table 2).

Guarantees#

  1. The Rasch difficulties have mean 0 before the correction; the 2PL’s abilities of the examinees kept have mean 0 and standard deviation 1.

  2. Examinees with no item or every item correct, and items answered correctly by every examinee or by none, are left out, repeatedly until neither is left; the items left out have NaN estimates, and the examinees left out the ability \(\infty\) or \(-\infty\).

  3. When a 2PL slope has no finite estimate, or an item step gives a slope that is not positive, the result holds the last complete cycle and warns with a ConvergenceWarning.

  4. A missing response raises an error that names MMLCalibrator as the alternative.

API Reference#

Joint maximum likelihood calibration of the Rasch model and the 2PL.

class irtlib.jml.JMLCalibrator(model: IrtModel | str = IrtModel.PL2, *, correct: bool | None = None, correct_errors: bool = False, tolerance: float = 1e-06, max_cycles: int = 500)[source]#

Calibrate the Rasch model or the 2PL by joint maximum likelihood.

Each call to calibrate() estimates the items of one complete response matrix together with the abilities of its examinees, with no assumption about their distribution. The estimates are not consistent for a test of fixed length; they serve comparisons of calibration methods and preliminary estimates. On short tests the 2PL’s joint maximum often does not exist, its slopes growing without bound; calibration then stops early with a ConvergenceWarning.

The Joint Maximum Likelihood page gives the method, its sources and the origin of each default.

Parameters:
modelirtlib.IrtModel or str, optional

"2PL", the default, estimates a slope and a difficulty per item, with the abilities given mean 0 and standard deviation 1; "1PL" the Rasch model, every slope 1, with the difficulties given mean 0. The 1PL of MMLCalibrator estimates one slope with abilities \(N(0, 1)\) instead, so the two compare only after a linear change of the ability scale (ADR-0008).

correctbool or None, optional

Whether to multiply the difficulties of the Rasch model by (M - 1) / M, for M items kept, once the likelihood is maximized. Default is None, which corrects the Rasch model and not the 2PL.

correct_errorsbool, optional

Whether to multiply the difficulties’ standard errors by (M - 1) / M too, when the difficulties are corrected. Default is False, which keeps the standard errors of the estimates before the correction.

tolerancefloat, optional

The stopping rule: calibration stops when no item parameter changes by more than this in a cycle, an item step and an ability step. Default is 1e-6.

max_cyclesint, optional

The most cycles a calibration runs before it stops unconverged. Default is 500.

Raises:
ValueError

If model is neither the 1PL nor the 2PL, correct is True for the 2PL, correct_errors is True where the difficulties are not corrected, tolerance is not positive, or max_cycles is less than 1.

Examples

>>> import numpy
>>> responses = numpy.array([[1, 0], [1, 0], [0, 1], [1, 1], [0, 0]], dtype=float)
>>> calibration = JMLCalibrator("1PL", correct=False).calibrate(responses)
>>> calibration.estimates[:, 1].round(4)  # b_2 - b_1 = 2 log(2 / 1)
array([-0.6931,  0.6931])
calibrate(responses: NDArray[floating[Any]]) → Calibration[source]#

Estimate the items of a complete response matrix, with the abilities of its examinees.

Examinees with no item or every item correct have no finite ability estimate and are left out, as are items answered correctly by every examinee or by none, repeatedly until neither is left.

Parameters:
responsesnumpy.ndarray

The response matrix: one row per examinee, one column per item, holding 1 or 0.

Returns:
Calibration

The estimates [a, b, 0, 1], their standard errors, and the joint log-likelihood at the estimates, before any correction, of the examinees and items kept; the result has no grid. An item left out has NaN estimates and standard errors, and the result’s bank leaves it out, with a RuntimeWarning naming it. The result’s abilities are every examinee’s, those of the cycle the estimates come from, on the same scale, with standard errors one over the square root of the test information at the estimates before any correction, and the evaluations of the ability step that gave them, 0 for starting values. An examinee left out has the ability inf or -inf, for every item kept right or wrong, standard error inf and 0 evaluations. Abilities at the corrected difficulties are irtlib.ability.maximum_likelihood()’s, called as maximum_likelihood(responses[:, kept], calibration.estimates[kept]) for the items with estimates, kept = ~numpy.isnan(calibration.estimates[:, 1]). If the stopping rule is not met within max_cycles, the 2PL’s abilities separate an item’s correct responses from its incorrect ones, or an item step gives a slope that is not positive, the result holds the last complete cycle, says so, and a ConvergenceWarning names the items by their columns; if an item’s information cannot be inverted, the result has no standard errors and a RuntimeWarning is issued.

Raises:
TypeError

If the responses are neither Booleans, integers nor floats.

ValueError

If the responses fail irtlib.calibration.validate_responses() or have a missing response; no item is left once examinees and items are left out; under the Rasch model, the joint likelihood has no maximum, which the error explains by the two groups of items the responses split into; under the 2PL, every examinee kept has the same number correct, which leaves the starting abilities no spread; or an item’s information is singular during the cycles.
