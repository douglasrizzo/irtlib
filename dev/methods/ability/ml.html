Maximum Likelihood#

API:

irtlib.ability.maximum_likelihood()

Sources:

Magis and Raîche [2012]; Warm [1989]; Chang and Ying [1996]; Veerkamp and Berger [1994]

Motivation#

The maximum likelihood (ML) estimate is the ability at which the examinee’s responses are most likely. It needs no prior, so it adds nothing to what the responses say, and its standard error comes straight from the test information. Its weakness is responses all correct or all incorrect, which no finite ability explains best; irtlib reports them as they are, \(+\infty\) or \(-\infty\), and lets the caller choose bounds or a fallback estimator. Maximum A Posteriori and Expected A Posteriori add a prior, and Weighted Likelihood removes the estimate’s first-order bias.

Definition#

The estimate maximizes \(L_j(\theta)\), and its standard error is \(1 / \sqrt{I(\hat\theta)}\) [Magis and Raîche, 2012, p. 4, Equations 2 and 3]. The likelihood of responses all correct rises with the ability and that of responses all incorrect falls, so their estimates are \(+\infty\) and \(-\infty\) [Warm, 1989, p. 442]. As \(\theta \to -\infty\) each \(P_i\) tends to \(c_i\), and as \(\theta \to +\infty\) to \(d_i\), so the likelihood tends to \(\prod_i c_i^{u_{ij}} (1 - c_i)^{1 - u_{ij}}\) and to \(\prod_i d_i^{u_{ij}} (1 - d_i)^{1 - u_{ij}}\): under the 3PL and 4PL, responses that are neither all correct nor all incorrect can also have their supremum at an infinity.

The maximum likelihood estimate is the maximizer over the extended real line: \(+\infty\) or \(-\infty\) wherever the likelihood comes closest to its supremum only in that limit, with standard error \(\infty\), since the information tends to 0 at either infinity (ADR-0010). An examinee who answered no item has a constant likelihood, which every ability maximizes: its estimate is NaN, and its standard error \(\infty\), the information being 0.

Implementation#

Bounds or a fallback. The caller has two ways to finite numbers, never used together:

  • bounds: the estimate maximizes the likelihood within them, and responses all correct or all incorrect give the bound they point to. Bounds are always set in practice [Warm, 1989, p. 442].

  • fallback: another estimator, called once on the rows whose estimate is not finite, the unanswered rows included, whose estimates, standard errors and evaluation counts replace theirs; the from_fallback field of AbilityEstimates marks those rows.

The search. Where an item answered has \(c_i > 0\) or \(d_i < 1\), the likelihood can have several maxima: under the 3PL the likelihood equation may have several roots [Chang and Ying, 1996, p. 223], and the likelihood several local maxima [Veerkamp and Berger, 1994, p. 4]. The search evaluates the log-likelihood at equally spaced abilities of the range: 101 when no item answered has a slope \(a_i\) above 2.5, 0.12 apart on \([-6, 6]\), and otherwise \(\lceil 40 \max_i a_i \rceil + 1\), 0.3 divided by the steepest slope apart on \([-6, 6]\), since a steep item can make a peak narrower than 0.12, up to 2,401 points, 0.005 apart, for slopes of 60 and above, so that a pathological slope cannot exhaust memory. It refines the best between its neighbours by Newton’s method on the log-likelihood’s first and second derivatives, from the vertex of the parabola through the three points, keeping each step inside a bracket that shrinks to the side the first derivative points to and bisecting the bracket where a step would leave it, and it keeps the refinement only if no worse. When every item answered has \(c_i = 0\) and \(d_i = 1\), the second derivative is \(-I\) (see Maximum A Posteriori), so the log-likelihood is concave and has at most one maximum. The search then skips the points: the estimate is a bound where the first derivative points out of the range, and otherwise the zero of the first derivative, found by the same bracketed Newton’s method from the middle of the range.

On about 38,000 random mixed patterns of 1 to 60 3PL and 4PL items with slopes from 0.5 to 2.5, half with responses drawn from the model, 101 and 201 points found the global maximum on \([-6, 6]\) every time and 81 points missed it once, while Brent’s method over the whole range, with no points, missed it 203 times, by a median of 3.7 in ability. On about 27,000 patterns of 1 to 30 items, half of them with slopes up to 60, the points spaced by the steepest slope missed it twice, each time by less than 0.001 in log-likelihood, where 101 points missed it 50 times and 201 points 11 (measured 2026-11-28). The numbers of points have no published source; the sources wanted list them.

Without bounds. The range starts as \([-6, 6]\). Beyond the lower end of the range, a correct response’s probability is at most its value at the end, since it rises with the ability, and an incorrect one’s at most \(1 - c_i\); beyond the upper end, a correct one’s is at most \(d_i\) and an incorrect one’s at most its value at the end. While these bounds leave room beyond an end for a log-likelihood above both the best found and the two limits, that end moves out, twice as far each time, and the new stretch is searched the same way, with as many points as the range, so that their spacing doubles with each stretch. The estimate is then the infinity of the larger limit, \(-\infty\) of two equal ones, if that limit is at least the best value found, within a relative \(10^{-12}\), and the best ability found otherwise. After 64 moves of an end, which only items of a vanishing discrimination need, the search stops at the best ability found and warns.

Comparison with mirt and catR

  • mirt 1.48 also gives \(+\infty\) or \(-\infty\) to responses all correct or all incorrect (R/fscores.internal.R:397-405), with no standard error, NA, where irtlib’s is \(\infty\), and NA to both for an examinee who answered no item (R/fscores.internal.R:94-96, 185). It searches the other responses with R’s nlm from the EAP estimate, up to an ability of \(\pm 20\) (R/fscores.R:305; R/fscores.internal.R:687), takes the standard error from nlm’s numerical Hessian, the observed information (R/fscores.internal.R:1072-1101), which is \(I\) for items with \(c_i = 0\) and \(d_i = 1\), and turns an estimate whose standard error exceeds 30 into an infinity (R/fscores.internal.R:1094-1096), which irtlib does not do.

  • catR 3.17 sets bounds by default: its default constantPatt = NULL estimates responses all correct or all incorrect by maximum likelihood too [Magis et al., 2022, p. 115], which returns the end of its range that the score points to (R/thetaEst.R:9-10, 100-109). Where the likelihood has a local minimum inside the range, catR’s thetaEst solves the likelihood equation with uniroot (R/thetaEst.R:100-102), which can converge to that minimum, while irtlib maximizes the likelihood: the parity tests keep those cases as expected failures (ADR-0003).

Guarantees#

  1. Responses all correct get \(+\infty\) and responses all incorrect \(-\infty\), with standard error \(\infty\), unless bounds or fallback is given.

  2. With bounds, the estimate of every examinee who answered an item lies within them.

  3. Where the likelihood has several maxima, the estimate is the highest the search’s points separate.

  4. An examinee who answered no item gets NaN, with standard error \(\infty\), unless fallback is given.

API Reference#

irtlib.ability.maximum_likelihood(responses: ArrayLike, items: NDArray[floating[Any]], *, bounds: tuple[float, float] | None = None, fallback: Callable[[NDArray[floating[Any]], NDArray[floating[Any]]], AbilityEstimates] | None = None, tol: float = 1e-06) → AbilityEstimates[source]#

Return each examinee’s maximum likelihood estimate, with its standard error.

Without bounds the estimate maximizes the likelihood over the whole ability scale, infinities included: it is inf or -inf where the likelihood comes closest to its supremum only in that limit, as it does for responses all correct or all incorrect, and its standard error is then inf. With bounds it maximizes the likelihood within them, and is finite. An examinee who answered no item has no estimate, NaN, and standard error inf. A fallback replaces every estimate that is not finite, infinite or NaN.

The Maximum Likelihood page gives the method, its sources and the origin of each default.

Parameters:
responsesarray_like

The response matrix, one row per examinee and one column per item: 1 or True for a correct response, 0 or False for an incorrect one, and NaN for a missing one.

itemsnumpy.ndarray

The items [a, b, c, d], a matrix of shape (n, 4), one row per column of responses, with a > 0 and 0 <= c < d <= 1.

boundstuple[float, float] or None, optional

The range the estimate is searched in; responses all correct or all incorrect give the bound they point to. Default is None, the whole ability scale.

fallbackCallable or None, optional

An estimator called as fallback(responses, items), once, on the rows whose estimate is not finite, such as functools.partial(expected_a_posteriori, log_prior=normal_log_prior(0.0, 1.0)); its estimates, standard errors and evaluation counts replace theirs, and from_fallback marks them. Default is None, which keeps them.

tolfloat, optional

The absolute tolerance of the search, in the ability scale. Default is 1e-6.

Returns:
AbilityEstimates

The estimates, their standard errors, and the number of evaluations of each examinee’s likelihood or of its derivatives.

Raises:
TypeError

If the responses are neither Booleans, integers nor floats.

ValueError

If both bounds and fallback are given, bounds is not a finite interval, tol is not positive, items is not an (n, 4) matrix, responses is not a matrix with one column per item, a response is not 0, 1 or NaN, or the fallback does not return one estimate per row it is given.

Examples

>>> import numpy
>>> items = numpy.array([[1.0, -1.0, 0.0, 1.0], [1.5, 0.0, 0.2, 1.0], [0.8, 1.0, 0.0, 0.95]])
>>> responses = numpy.array([[1, 0, numpy.nan], [1, 1, 1]])
>>> maximum_likelihood(responses, items).theta.round(4)
array([-0.6463,     inf])
>>> maximum_likelihood(responses, items, bounds=(-4.0, 4.0)).theta.round(4)
array([-0.6463,  4.    ])
>>> estimates = maximum_likelihood(responses, items, fallback=expected_a_posteriori)
>>> estimates.theta.round(4), estimates.from_fallback
(array([-0.6463,  0.8555]), array([False,  True]))
